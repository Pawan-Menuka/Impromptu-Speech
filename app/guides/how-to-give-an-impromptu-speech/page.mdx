# How to Give an Impromptu Speech (Step by Step)

An impromptu speech is a talk you give with little or no preparation — usually 15–30 seconds to think, then
1–2 minutes to speak. The fastest way to get good at one isn't to think faster; it's to stop trying to plan
*what* you'll say and instead plan *how* you'll say it. A simple, repeatable structure carries almost any
topic, and it's the difference between a speech that wanders and one that lands.

## What counts as an impromptu speech?

If you've done a Toastmasters "table topics" round, answered an unexpected question in an interview, been
called on in a meeting with no warning, or drawn a random prompt in a class assignment, you've given an
impromptu speech. The common thread is time pressure: little or no prep, and a short window to speak — usually
30 seconds to 2 minutes.

## Step 1: use your prep time to plan structure, not content

With 15–30 seconds, you cannot plan every sentence, and trying to is what causes the freeze. Instead, spend
that time on three things only: pick your structure, decide your opening line, and decide your closing line.
Everything in between gets filled in as you speak, because you already know the shape it needs to take.

## Step 2: pick one simple structure

The most reliable structure for a short impromptu answer is **PREP**:

- **Point** — your one-sentence answer or position
- **Reason** — why
- **Example** — a short, concrete illustration
- **Point** — restate it, to close

> "Should schools require public speaking classes? Yes — because the two skills that scare people most,
> speaking clearly under pressure and thinking on your feet, are exactly what most curriculums never teach.
> I've watched people freeze in meetings over questions they knew the answer to, purely because they'd never
> practiced answering out loud. That's why schools should require it."

Four sentences, and it holds together. For longer or more complex topics, two other structures work well:
**Past-Present-Future** (how did we get here, where are we now, where does this go) and
**Problem-Solution** (what's the problem, why it matters, what would fix it). Pick whichever fits the
question fastest — don't spend prep time debating between them.

## Step 3: open with your point, not a preamble

The most common way an impromptu speech goes wrong is the opening: "So, um, that's an interesting question,
I guess I'd say..." Every one of those words is prep time spent on air, not content. State your point in the
first sentence. It sounds more confident, and it forces you to actually decide your position before you start
talking instead of discovering it mid-sentence.

## Step 4: fill the middle with one reason and one example

Resist the urge to list three reasons, four examples, and a caveat. One clear reason and one concrete example
is enough for a short speech, and it's far easier to deliver without losing your thread. If you have more
prep time or a longer time limit, add a second reason — but only after the first one is fully said, not as a
parallel thought competing for the same sentence.

## Step 5: close by restating your point

Don't just stop talking when you run out of things to say — that's what makes an ending feel like it
trailed off. Restate your point, ideally in slightly different words than your opening, and stop. A speech
that opens and closes on the same idea reads as intentional, even if everything in between was improvised.

## How long should an impromptu speech be?

For table topics and most classroom or interview contexts, 1–2 minutes is standard. At that length, PREP's
four beats map naturally to roughly 15–20 seconds each. Shorter formats (30–60 seconds) usually drop to just
Point–Reason–Point; longer ones (3+ minutes) can support two reasons or a full Past-Present-Future arc.

## Common mistakes that sink impromptu speeches

- **Rambling into the topic** instead of stating a position — the audience doesn't know where you're headed
  until you tell them.
- **Filler words** (um, uh, like, you know) filling the thinking gaps instead of a brief, comfortable pause.
  A short silence reads as composed; a filler word reads as lost.
- **Trying to cover everything** — three half-finished reasons are weaker than one fully-delivered one.
- **No close** — stopping mid-thought rather than restating the point, which is what makes the ending feel
  unplanned even when the middle was strong.

## How to get better at impromptu speaking

Structure is a skill you build by repetition, not by reading about it. A useful routine:

1. Get a random prompt — pick one from the [topic library](/topics), sorted by difficulty, or use the
   [random topic generator](/topic-generator) for a quick, unscripted pick.
2. Give yourself real prep time — 15–30 seconds, timed, not open-ended.
3. Speak for 1–2 minutes, out loud, recorded if you can.
4. Listen back (or get feedback) specifically for structure — did you open with your point, was there one
   clear reason and example, did you close on purpose — separately from how it felt to deliver.

That last step is the one people skip, because it's uncomfortable to review your own unscripted speaking. It's
also where the actual improvement happens.
